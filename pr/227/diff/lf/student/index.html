9+-- ######################################################################
10+-- Welcome
11+
12+-- This is Lean-based entry point to a series of electronic textbooks on
13+-- various aspects of *Software Foundations*, the mathematical underpinnings
14+-- of reliable software. Topics in the series include basic concepts of logic,
15+-- computer-assisted theorem proving, the Lean prover, functional programming,
16+-- operational semantics, logics and techniques for reasoning about programs,
17+-- static type systems, property-based random testing, and verification of
18+-- practical C code. The exposition is intended for a broad range of readers,
19+-- from advanced undergraduates to PhD students and researchers. No specific
20+-- background in logic or programming languages is assumed, though a degree of
21+-- mathematical maturity will be helpful.
22+
23+-- It is important to note that the *Software Foundations* series is
24+-- originally written using the *Rocq* proof assistant-- another theorem
25+-- prover which is similar to but distinct from Lean. This book marks the
26+-- first effort in translating the Rocq-based series to Lean-- an effort that
27+-- will (hopefully!) continue with the generous support of the Lean and Rocq
28+-- communities.
29+
30+-- The principal novelty of the series is that it is one hundred percent
31+-- formalized and machine-checked: each text is literally a script for Lean.
32+-- The books are intended to be read alongside (or inside) an interactive
33+-- session with Lean. All the details in the text are fully formalized in
34+-- Lean, and most of the exercises are designed to be worked using Lean.
35+
36+-- The files in each book are organized into a sequence of core chapters,
37+-- covering about one semester's worth of material and organized into a
38+-- coherent linear narrative, plus a number of "offshoot" chapters covering
39+-- additional topics. All the core chapters are suitable for both upper-level
40+-- undergraduate and graduate students.
41+
42+-- This book, *Logical Foundations*, lays groundwork for the others,
43+-- introducing the reader to the basic ideas of functional programming,
44+-- constructive logic, and the Lean prover.
45+
46+-- ######################################################################
47+-- Overview
48+
49+-- Building reliable software is hard -- really hard. The scale and complexity
50+-- of modern systems, the number of people involved, and the range of demands
51+-- placed on them make it challenging to build software that is even
52+-- more-or-less correct, much less 100%% correct. At the same time, the
53+-- increasing degree to which information processing is woven into every
54+-- aspect of society greatly amplifies the cost of bugs and insecurities.
55+
56+-- Computer scientists and software engineers have responded to these
57+-- challenges by developing a host of techniques for improving software
58+-- reliability, ranging from recommendations about managing software projects
59+-- teams (e.g., extreme programming) to design philosophies for libraries
60+-- (e.g., model-view-controller, publish-subscribe, etc.) and programming
61+-- languages (e.g., object-oriented programming, functional programming, ...)
62+-- to mathematical techniques for specifying and reasoning about properties of
63+-- software and tools for helping validate these properties. The *Software
64+-- Foundations* series is focused on this last set of tools.
65+
66+-- This volume weaves together three conceptual threads:
67+
68+-- (A) basic tools from *logic* for making and justifying precise claims about
69+-- programs;
70+
71+-- (B) the use of *proof assistants* (or *provers*) to construct rigorous
72+-- logical arguments;
73+
74+-- (C) *functional programming*, both as a method of programming that
75+-- simplifies reasoning about programs and as a bridge between programming and
76+-- logic.
77+
78+-- Logic
79+
80+-- Logic is the field of study whose subject matter is *proofs* --
81+-- unassailable arguments for the truth of particular propositions. Volumes
82+-- have been written about the central role of logic in computer science.
83+-- Manna and Waldinger called it "the calculus of computer science," while
84+-- Halpern et al.'s paper *On the Unusual Effectiveness of Logic in Computer
85+-- Science* catalogs scores of ways in which logic offers critical tools and
86+-- insights. Indeed, they observe that, "As a matter of fact, logic has turned
87+-- out to be significantly more effective in computer science than it has been
88+-- in mathematics. This is quite remarkable, especially since much of the
89+-- impetus for the development of logic during the past one hundred years came
90+-- from mathematics."
91+
92+-- In particular, the fundamental tools of *inductive proof* are ubiquitous in
93+-- all of computer science. You have surely seen them before, perhaps in a
94+-- course on discrete math or analysis of algorithms, but in this course we
95+-- will examine them more deeply than you have probably done so far.
96+
97+-- Proof Assistants
98+
99+-- The flow of ideas between logic and computer science has not been
100+-- unidirectional: CS has also made important contributions to logic. One of
101+-- these has been the development of software tools for helping construct
102+-- proofs of logical propositions. These tools fall into two broad categories:
103+
104+-- - *Automated theorem provers* provide "push-button" operation: you give them
105+--   a proposition and they return either *true* or *false* (or, sometimes,
106+--   *don't know: ran out of time*). Although their reasoning capabilities are
107+--   still limited, they have matured tremendously in recent decades and are
108+--   used now in a multitude of settings. Examples of such tools include SAT
109+--   solvers, SMT solvers, and model checkers.
110+
111+-- - *Proof assistants* are hybrid tools that automate the more routine aspects
112+--   of building proofs while depending on human guidance for more difficult
113+--   aspects. Widely used proof assistants include Isabelle, Agda, Twelf, ACL2,
114+--   PVS, F⋆, HOL4, Rocq, and Lean, among many others.
115+
116+-- This course is based around Lean, a proof assistant that has been under
117+-- development since 2013 and has attracted a large community of users in both
118+-- research and industry. Lean provides a rich environment for interactive
119+-- development of machine-checked formal reasoning. The kernel of the Lean
120+-- system is a simple proof-checker, which guarantees that only correct
121+-- deduction steps are ever performed. On top of this kernel, the Lean
122+-- environment provides high-level facilities for proof development, including
123+-- a large library of common definitions and lemmas, powerful tactics for
124+-- constructing complex proofs semi-automatically, and a special-purpose
125+-- programming language for defining new proof-automation tactics for specific
126+-- situations.
127+
128+-- Lean has been a critical enabler for a huge variety of work across computer
129+-- science and mathematics:

151+-- The term *functional programming* refers both to a collection of
152+-- programming idioms that can be used in almost any programming language and
153+-- to a family of programming languages designed to emphasize these idioms,
154+-- including Haskell, OCaml, Standard ML, F##, Scala, Scheme, Racket, Common
155+-- Lisp, Clojure, Erlang, F⋆, and Lean.
156+
157+-- Functional programming has been developed over many decades -- indeed, its
158+-- roots go back to Church's lambda-calculus, which was invented in the 1930s,
159+-- well *before* the first electronic computers! But since the early '90s it
160+-- has enjoyed a surge of interest among industrial engineers and language
161+-- designers, playing a key role in high-value systems at companies like Jane
162+-- Street Capital, Microsoft, Facebook, Twitter, and Ericsson.
163+
164+-- The most basic tenet of functional programming is that, as much as
165+-- possible, computation should be *pure*, in the sense that the only effect
166+-- of execution should be to produce a result: it should be free from *side
167+-- effects* such as I/O, assignments to mutable variables, redirecting
168+-- pointers, etc. For example, whereas an *imperative* sorting function might
169+-- take a list of numbers and rearrange its pointers to put the list in order,
170+-- a pure sorting function would take the original list and return a *new*
171+-- list containing the same numbers in sorted order.
172+
173+-- A significant benefit of this style of programming is that it makes
174+-- programs easier to understand and reason about. If every operation on a
175+-- data structure yields a new data structure, leaving the old one intact,
176+-- then there is no need to worry about how that structure is being shared and
177+-- whether a change by one part of the program might break an invariant relied
178+-- on by another part of the program. These considerations are particularly
179+-- critical in concurrent systems, where every piece of mutable state that is
180+-- shared between threads is a potential source of pernicious bugs. Indeed, a
181+-- large part of the recent interest in functional programming in industry is
182+-- due to its simpler behavior in the presence of concurrency.
183+
184+-- Another reason for the current excitement about functional programming is
185+-- related to the first: functional programs are often much easier to
186+-- parallelize and physically distribute than their imperative counterparts.
187+-- If running a computation has no effect other than producing a result, then
188+-- it does not matter *where* it is run. Similarly, if a data structure is
189+-- never modified destructively, then it can be copied freely, across cores or
190+-- across the network. Indeed, the "Map-Reduce" idiom, which lies at the heart
191+-- of massively distributed query processors like Hadoop and is used by Google
192+-- to index the entire web is a classic example of functional programming.
193+
194+-- For purposes of this course, functional programming has yet another
195+-- significant attraction: it serves as a bridge between logic and computer
196+-- science. Indeed, Lean itself can be viewed as a combination an extremely
197+-- expressive functional programming language plus a set of tools for stating
198+-- and proving logical assertions. Moreover, when we come to look more
199+-- closely, we find that these two sides of Lean are actually aspects of the
200+-- very same underlying machinery -- i.e., *proofs are programs*.

243+-- Exercises
244+
245+-- Each chapter includes numerous exercises. Each is marked with a "star
246+-- rating," which can be interpreted as follows:
247+
248+-- - One star: easy exercises that underscore points in the text and that, for
249+--   most readers, should take only a minute or two. Get in the habit of working
250+--   these as you reach them.
251+
252+-- - Two stars: straightforward exercises (five or ten minutes).
253+
254+-- - Three stars: exercises requiring a bit of thought (ten minutes to half an
255+--   hour).
256+
257+-- - Four and five stars: more difficult exercises (half an hour and up).
258+
259+-- Those using SF in a classroom setting should note that the autograder
260+-- assigns extra points to harder exercises:
261+
262+-- 1 star  = 1 point
263+--       2 stars = 2 points
264+--       3 stars = 3 points
265+--       4 stars = 6 points
266+--       5 stars = 10 points
267+
268+-- Some exercises are marked "advanced," and some are marked "optional." Doing
269+-- just the non-optional, non-advanced exercises should provide good coverage
270+-- of the core material. Optional exercises provide a bit of extra practice
271+-- with key concepts and introduce secondary themes that may be of interest to
272+-- some readers. Advanced exercises are for readers who want an extra
273+-- challenge and a deeper cut at the material.
